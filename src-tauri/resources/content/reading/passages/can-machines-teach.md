title: Can Machines Teach Us to Think?
level: b2-mastery

---

When the first tutoring programs appeared in schools, many teachers feared they would be replaced. The software could mark quizzes in seconds, explain grammar rules patiently, and repeat a lesson as many times as a student wished. [[GAP:1]] A decade later, the results are more complicated than either the enthusiasts or the critics predicted.

The clearest gains have appeared in routine practice. Students learning vocabulary or basic mathematics benefit from endless exercises that adjust to their level. A program notices after three mistakes that a learner confuses two similar words, and it quietly schedules extra review the next day. No human teacher, however dedicated, can track the errors of thirty students with such precision. Test scores in these narrow skills have improved in school after school.

Yet thinking is more than remembering correct answers. [[GAP:2]] In classroom discussions, students must defend an opinion, listen to objections, and change their minds in front of others. These moments feel uncomfortable, and that discomfort is exactly the point. A machine can simulate a debate partner, but students know it will never judge them, and so they take fewer intellectual risks. Researchers who compared machine-tutored groups with discussion-based classes found that the second group wrote stronger essays, even when their quiz scores were lower.

The most promising schools have therefore stopped asking whether machines can replace teachers and started asking how the two can divide the work. [[GAP:3]] In these classrooms, software handles nightly drills and instant feedback, while morning lessons are reserved for argument, storytelling, and questions without a single correct answer. Teachers report that they finally have time to do what drew them to the profession: listening carefully to how a young mind reasons.

There are warnings worth hearing. Poorer schools often receive only the software, without the smaller discussion groups that make it effective, which widens the gap the technology promised to close. Privacy is another concern, since learning platforms collect detailed records of every hesitation and mistake a child makes. [[GAP:4]] Regulators in several countries now require schools to explain what data is stored and for how long.

Perhaps the right question was never whether machines can teach. They can, and they already do. The harder question is what kind of thinking we want education to protect. If schools use machines to free time for curiosity and judgement, technology becomes an ally. If they use machines merely to cut costs, classrooms may produce faster answers and slower minds.
