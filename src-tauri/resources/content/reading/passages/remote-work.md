Ten years ago, working from home was a rare privilege. Today, millions of people begin their working day by opening a laptop at the kitchen table. [[GAP:1]] This shift has changed not only where we work, but also how companies measure success.

Supporters of remote work point to clear benefits. Without a daily commute, employees save time and money, and many report higher concentration in a quiet home office. Parents in particular value the freedom to organise their timetable around school hours and family meals. Companies, meanwhile, can hire talented staff from any city and spend less on large offices. Several studies suggest that productivity stays the same or even rises when people choose their own workplace, especially for tasks that require deep focus.

However, isolation remains a serious challenge. [[GAP:2]] Young employees especially say they miss the informal conversations that used to happen near the coffee machine. These small moments often carried useful advice, quick feedback, and friendship. When every meeting becomes a scheduled video call, such moments quietly disappear.

Managers have responded in different ways. Some demand a full return to the office, arguing that creativity needs face-to-face contact. They organise team lunches and workshops to rebuild the office culture they feel has weakened. Others accept a hybrid model, where staff spend two or three days at home and the rest with colleagues. [[GAP:3]] The second approach is now the most common, particularly in technology and education.

The debate is unlikely to end soon. What seems certain is that work will never return to its old shape entirely. The laptop, the video call, and the flexible timetable have become permanent parts of professional life, and both workers and companies are still learning how to use them well.
