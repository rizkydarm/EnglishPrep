In many crowded cities, fresh vegetables travel hundreds of kilometres before reaching the shops. By the time they arrive, they are expensive and far less fresh. [[GAP:1]] On the flat roofs of offices, schools, and apartment blocks, residents now grow spinach, tomatoes, chillies, and herbs for their own neighbourhoods.

The idea began with small community groups. Volunteers carried soil up the stairs in sacks, built simple wooden boxes, and planted the first seeds. At first, neighbours doubted the harvest would be worth the effort. Yet within one season, a single rooftop garden produced enough greens for twenty families, and the project quickly spread to nearby buildings.

Rooftop farms bring benefits beyond food. Plants cool the building below, which reduces the need for air conditioning during hot months. Residents on the top floor notice that their rooms stay comfortable even in the dry season. [[GAP:2]] Rainwater collected in tanks waters the beds, so the gardens use far less treated water than ordinary farms. The roofs have also become social spaces where children learn how food grows and elderly residents meet their neighbours.

There are real difficulties, of course. Strong wind can damage young plants, and carrying materials upstairs is heavy work. Volunteers must also agree on who waters the beds during holidays, which is not always easy. Some buildings need engineers to check that the roof can hold the extra weight of soil and water. [[GAP:3]] These problems are solvable, but they require planning and money.

Despite the challenges, supporters believe rooftop farming will keep growing. It cannot feed an entire city, but it shortens the journey from soil to plate, cuts pollution from transport, and reminds city dwellers that fresh food does not have to come from far away.
